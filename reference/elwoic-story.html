Project History

The Story of ELWOIC

A student-led effort to build hyperlocal weather and environmental observation in Elamkulam, Kerala.

Prepared by Aswanth Krishna K. Founder & Operator

Statement of Purpose

ELWOIC exists solely for the benefit of the community. As an independent initiative, all efforts are directed toward improving access to accurate weather information and enhancing public awareness in Elamkulam. Our mission is simple: "Observe what is happening in Elamkulam, understand it, and make that information available to the people who live here."

1. The Need for True Local Data

My interest in the environment began in the 10th standard. I was deeply curious about how weather conditions shift from place to place and what it meant for the natural world around me. By the time I reached my 11th standard, this curiosity had hardened into a specific mission: I wanted a dedicated place where the people of Elamkulam could freely find accurate, local weather and environmental information to make daily decisions.

What does ELWOIC stand for?

  • Elamkulam — the locality the project is focused on.
  • Local — the emphasis on information specific to the area.
  • Weather — weather conditions and observations.
  • Observation — monitoring, analysing, studying, and understanding.
  • Information — the knowledge people can receive from the system.
  • Centre — a dedicated place for this information.

Initially, ELWOIC was just a small Google Sites page aggregating public API data. But I quickly realized a critical flaw: a forecast covering a wider regional boundary doesn't represent exact local conditions. It might be pouring rain 15 kilometres away, yet completely dry in Elamkulam. I realized that to be truly useful, ELWOIC needed to stop displaying distant data and start measuring actual conditions on the ground.

2. The First Breakthrough & The Customs Struggle

Realizing that the AQI information I was pulling from external sources didn't match what I observed outside, I began searching for environmental monitoring equipment. As a student with limited funds, I reached out to organizations hoping for support. To my absolute surprise, AirGradient responded positively and agreed to sponsor ELWOIC with an Open Air O-1PST monitor.

But receiving it was a harsh introduction to international logistics. When the device arrived via DHL, customs slapped a ₹3,000 import charge on it. ELWOIC had no funding. Convincing my parents to pay this was incredibly difficult; from their perspective, paying a hefty import fee for a "free" sponsored device didn't directly benefit our daily lives. But I knew that if I abandoned this opportunity over a customs fee, the project would die before it even started. My parents eventually supported me, and I paid the fee personally.

The device arrived in parts on February 24, 2026. My father and I navigated the assembly together—even getting briefly confused by a foil-covered component meant for drawing in air. When we powered it on inside the house, it read around 40 μg/m³. A few days later, I moved it outside to a real residential environment—a rooftop more than 10 feet above ground—and the readings settled into the 30 μg/m³ range. Having continuous, real measurements brought a profound sense of trust and proof to the project.

3. The UPS Maze and Ecowitt

AirGradient's support gave me confidence. I emailed five or six weather station manufacturers. Only one replied: Ecowitt. They generously agreed to sponsor our setup with their GW2000A0B133 Wittboy weather station.

Learning from the DHL experience, I requested Ecowitt to declare it as educational equipment and ship it via an economy courier. They used UPS, which successfully kept the customs charges down to around ₹1,800, but the clearance process became a bureaucratic nightmare. First, the KYC submission stalled because I did not have a PAN card, and my father had valid security concerns about providing his. Once cleared, the payment was delayed. I had to make endless phone calls to UPS just to get the payment amount and paperwork so I could pay manually.

Even after the payment was cleared by higher-ups, the warehouse workers refused to release the package, demanding their own internal payment verifications. When it finally reached my town via a secondary courier, they refused to deliver it to my home. I had to travel into town to physically retrieve it. It was a massive journey of emails, phone calls, and sheer persistence.

4. Security Paranoia and Community Rejections

Securing the hardware was only half the battle; finding a location was harder. I needed a high, open, and clear space. On the very last day of my 12th-standard public exams, I approached the Principal of my school, GHSS Kunnakkavu. I explained ELWOIC and asked if we could place the Ecowitt station on the school roof. Caught in the academic rush, he told me to come back the following week.

I returned with a formal request letter. The Principal's reaction was essentially:

"Him again? What do I do with this?"

Another teacher stepped in to interrogate the idea. Despite my explanations, they treated the weather station like it was an advanced 360-degree AI CCTV camera capable of recording sound and motion. They cited strict government building rules, vague security concerns, and risks involving the PTA. A few days later, the Principal gave me a flat rejection.

I then approached the owners of homes situated in the highest parts of Elamkulam. Two homes had no WiFi connection at all. The one owner who did have connectivity rejected the idea due to the exact same paranoia about data security. I was locked out of the best geographical spots in town.

5. Building the 5-Meter Stand

Faced with rejection from the community, I fell back to the only place I had left: my own home. However, my house is surrounded by distant trees, meaning I needed significant height to capture accurate wind and rain data.

My father and I bought two 3-meter iron pipes designed to telescope into one another. We spent a considerable amount of time at a local engineering workshop, cutting, welding, and customizing the pipes until we had constructed a rigid stand standing over 5 meters tall. With intense physical effort, we hauled it home, hoisted it onto the roof, and secured it against the wind. I connected the station directly to my Jio AirFiber network, and finally, it was online.

6. Navigating Bureaucracy: Beetel and the Panchayat

For ELWOIC to function as a community centre, it needed a reliable contact line. A physical landline wasn't possible at my location, so I reached out to Beetel. A Kerala representative arranged a Beetel F1 4G Fixed Wireless Phone. The courier outsourced the delivery to a tiny, hidden office in a dense part of town. I arrived at 9:30 AM to find it locked. After calling the delivery driver, who had to track down the office manager (who wasn't answering his phone), I simply stood there and waited until 10:00 AM when someone finally showed up to hand me the box.

Getting official acknowledgment from the local government was a similar test of patience. I emailed the Panchayat—no reply. I sent a physical letter via post—no reply. Finally, I walked directly into the Panchayat office and submitted the request in person. The officer admitted he had seen both my email and my letter, but said nothing could be done without convening a Grama Sabha meeting. I waited weeks. The Sabha approved the project, but the clerk simply sat on the paperwork. I had to continually pester the officer and visit the office repeatedly until they finally handed me the official acknowledgment letter around January 2026.

In contrast, applying for the official IMD (India Meteorological Department) API through their portal was incredibly smooth, allowing me to display verified, official regional data right alongside our hyper-local readings on the website.

7. The Future: We Keep Moving

Today, this hardware runs continuously. The data flows from the Ecowitt and AirGradient monitors, through a Cloudflare-managed layer, and into a custom JavaScript system I developed with AI programming assistance. It powers the live dashboard, stores historical records, and automates community WhatsApp alerts.

My ideal vision is to establish monitoring points towards the North, South, East, and West of Elamkulam to track approaching weather from all directions. But more importantly, the journey of building ELWOIC has shaped how I view challenges.

"I don't know what ELWOIC will become in the coming years, but I continuously search for what it can be. I continuously search for new things and try to adapt and grow. There are many rejections and blocking elements in my path, but I don't stop there. I look for the next path after taking a short break. There might be a door for us. Keep moving. I believe the future is not predictable, therefore I keep moving with what I have right now and what can have in future."
